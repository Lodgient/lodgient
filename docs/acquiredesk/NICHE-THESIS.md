# Niche Thesis — Where We Hunt

Companion to `SPEC.md`. This document picks the vertical and explains why. The
product spec's buy box, scoring weights, and AI upside library should all be
specialized to whatever this document concludes.

**Status:** proposed. Quantitative claims marked ⚠ require verification before
capital is committed — they are informed estimates, not researched facts.

---

## 0. THE OPERATING MODEL, CORRECTED

Stated plan: acquire, spend three months installing AI, move to the next one.

The missing piece: **you still own it in month four.** "Move on" means "hand off,"
and the handoff is the binding constraint on this entire strategy — not deal
flow, not capital, not the AI work.

This has three consequences that drive niche selection:

**1. The work must be checklist-executable.** If doing the job correctly requires
diagnostic judgment — HVAC troubleshooting, plumbing, electrical, auto repair —
then the technician holds the leverage, is scarce, and is expensive. You are
buying a recruiting problem. If the job is *procedural* — inspect against a code
checklist, treat on a schedule, test and document — you can hire at market wage,
train in weeks, and promote a lead tech into a working GM.

**2. The AI system must become the management layer.** The point of the three
months is not cost savings. It is to make the business legible enough that a
$70–90k working GM can run it, because the system tells them what to do. That
lowers the caliber of operator required, which is the whole reason this scales.

**3. One niche, because the playbook is the compounding asset.** Deal 2 in the
same vertical reuses: the software, the SOPs, the licensing qualifier, the back
office, the vendor accounts, the recruiting pipeline, and the seller network
(operators in a niche all know each other — your best deal-two lead is your
deal-one seller). Deal 2 in a *new* vertical reuses none of it.

**Realistic cadence.** Deal 1 takes six months, not three, because deal 1 is
where the playbook gets written and it will be twice the work you expect. Deal 2
takes four and tells you whether you have a playbook or a coincidence. Deals 3+
approach three months. Plan for that, or you will conclude the model is broken
when in fact deal 1 was just deal 1.

## 1. WHAT MAKES A GOLDMINE

Ten criteria, in rough order of importance for *this* strategy.

**1. Mandated demand.** The customer does not choose *whether* to buy, only *from
whom*. Fire code, health code, insurance requirement, licensing condition. This
is the single strongest attribute available in small business: churn approaches
zero, price sensitivity is low, and recessions barely register. A business the
customer is legally required to buy from someone is categorically better than a
business the customer merely likes.

**2. A license moat we can satisfy but most buyers cannot.** This is the most
underrated criterion and it is nearly the entire edge. A licensing requirement
disqualifies the majority of the buyer pool for *every deal in the vertical* —
which suppresses competition and therefore price. But for us, satisfying it is a
**one-time cost amortized across five acquisitions.** Hire or partner with one
qualifier, and every subsequent deal in that niche is structurally less contested
for us and only for us. Nothing else on this list moves winnability that much.

**3. An AI lever that is large relative to EBITDA, not in absolute terms.** In a
business with $220k SDE, finding $60k is a 27% EBITDA lift and roughly a 27%
valuation lift. Cost-cutting cannot deliver that at this scale — there is not
enough overhead. It has to come from **recovering revenue the business already
earned the right to and never collected.** Which means the ideal niche has a
documented, structural revenue leak.

**4. Route density economics.** Revenue per drive-mile rises with local
customer count. This makes each acquisition worth more *to us* than to an
outside buyer, because we can absorb it into an existing route. It is the honest
version of synergy.

**5. Procedural, not diagnostic, labor.** See §0.

**6. Deal availability at our size, in our geography.** A perfect niche with four
targets in Florida is not a niche, it is a single opportunity. We need 30+
credible targets within 60 minutes to run a five-deal program.

**7. Entry multiple below exit multiple.** Fragmented at the bottom, consolidating
at the top. Buy at 2.5–3.5×, sell a platform at 6–9×. **Multiple expansion from
consolidation is a bigger number than the AI improvement**, and it requires no
technology at all. The AI is what makes the platform worth the higher multiple
and funds the debt along the way.

**8. B2B, contracted, invoiced.** Facility managers and property managers buy on
reliability and paperwork, not price. Consumers buy on price and churn.

**9. Low capex, low inventory.** Vacuum trucks and shred trucks are $200k+ each.
That kills a low-capital acquisition thesis before it starts.

**10. Owner is not the salesperson.** In mandated-service niches this is usually
true by default — the work renews itself.

### Notes on the losers — several were top picks in the v1 spec

**Pool service (v1's headline example).** Route businesses trade at a
well-established, widely-known multiple of monthly billings. It is a *liquid*
market with transparent pricing, which means **no information asymmetry to
exploit.** Add: consumer customers, price-sensitive, meaningful churn, near-zero
licensing barrier, and hurricane exposure. It is a fine business and a bad
strategy. Downgrade it out of the buy box.

**Commercial cleaning.** Zero licensing barrier means anyone with a van competes.
Bid-driven, thin margins, and the single worst labor turnover in services. Cheap
to buy for a reason. The AI lever is weak because the problem is labor
management, not information.

**Hood cleaning.** Genuinely mandated demand (NFPA 96), which is why it scores
well on the top line — but night work, greasy, brutal turnover, and the customer
base is restaurants, which v1 correctly said to avoid. Mandated demand does not
rescue a bad labor model.

**HVAC.** Excellent business, wrong moment. Private equity has been consolidating
residential and commercial HVAC aggressively; entry multiples are inflated well
past what our thesis tolerates ⚠, and technician scarcity in Florida is severe.
You would be paying a premium to acquire a recruiting problem.

**Grease trap and document destruction.** Both fail on capex alone. Vacuum trucks
and shred trucks are six figures each and financing them is a different business.

**Backflow testing.** Scores well on fundamentals — the *water utility* mails
your customer their renewal notice, which is close to a perfect demand mechanism.
It fails on deal availability: pure-play backflow shops at $150k+ SDE are rare;
it is nearly always a bolt-on. **Keep it as a tuck-in service line, not a
platform.**

## 3. THE PICK — FIRE & LIFE SAFETY INSPECTION AND SERVICE

Extinguishers, alarm and detection testing, sprinkler inspection (NFPA 25),
emergency and exit lighting, kitchen suppression systems, fire door inspection,
and the deficiency repair work that follows all of it.

### 3.1 Why this is the goldmine

**The customer physically cannot say no.** A building with an expired tag fails
its fire marshal inspection. Insurance requires current documentation.
Certificates of occupancy depend on it. This is not "sticky recurring revenue" —
it is legally compelled recurring revenue with a fixed calendar. No other niche
on the list matches it.

**There is a documented, structural revenue leak, and it is enormous.** Two of
them:

*(a) The lapsed-due-date base.* Every device has a next-due date defined by code —
annual, semi-annual, quarterly, monthly, five-year, six-year, twelve-year
depending on device and standard. Small operators track this on a spreadsheet or
in the owner's head. Customers who don't call don't get serviced, and quietly
lapse. **A significant share of a typical small shop's historical customer base
is sitting dormant with overdue devices** ⚠ — customers who are currently out of
compliance, still legally required to fix it, and simply were never called.

Recovering them is not selling. It is calling someone to tell them they are out
of compliance. Close rates on that call are unlike anything in normal sales, and
the incremental margin approaches 90% because the truck is already going past.

*(b) Deficiency-to-quote conversion — the real prize.* Every inspection produces
a deficiency report: this extinguisher needs hydrostatic testing, this head is
painted over, this backflow failed, this exit sign has a dead battery. The
correction work is typically **two to four times the inspection revenue** ⚠, it
is legally required, and the customer has already been told in writing that they
need it.

Small operators convert a fraction of it — often well under half ⚠ — for the
mundane reason that the inspector hands in a paper form and nobody follows up.

**This is the single best AI target I have seen in small-business services**, and
the reason is that the input is already a structured document that already
exists. You are not predicting anything. You are not generating demand. You are
taking a written list of legally-required work the customer has already been
informed of, and following up on it reliably. Deficiency → priced quote →
scheduled follow-up → escalation → close, automated, with the technician's photo
attached.

Nothing else in the candidate set has a revenue leak that is *this* large, *this*
documented, and *this* mechanical to fix.

**The licensing moat compounds across the roll-up.** Florida regulates fire
equipment dealers and fire protection contractors under Fla. Stat. Ch. 633, with
several license classes covering different scopes ⚠ (exact classes must be
confirmed with the State Fire Marshal's office before deal 1 — this is
non-negotiable pre-work, not diligence). The requirement disqualifies most
individual buyers. For us it is one qualifier relationship covering deals 1
through 5. **This is precisely the "solvable friction" the Winnability score in
SPEC.md §2.3 is designed to reward, and this niche is where that mechanic pays.**

**The compliance paperwork *is* the product.** Property and facility managers
choose vendors on whether the documentation arrives, is correct, and is findable
when the fire marshal asks. This is the rare case where the AI upgrade —
instant, clean, searchable, photo-documented reports delivered before the truck
leaves the lot — is simultaneously a cost saving *and* the primary sales
differentiator. In most niches the AI work is invisible to the customer. Here it
is the reason they switch to you and stay.

**The exit is defined and the multiple gap is wide.** Fire and life safety is
being actively consolidated by national platforms ⚠. That gives us three things:
a proven roll-up model, a known buyer set, and a multiple gap between what a
single-truck shop trades at and what an integrated regional platform trades at.

**The multiple arbitrage is larger than the AI gain, and it is more certain.**
Rough shape of the five-deal program:

```
Acquire 4–5 shops averaging ~$250k SDE at ~3× ......  ~$1.1M combined EBITDA
Improve each ~25–30% (recovery + deficiency + price)   ~$1.4M combined EBITDA
Exit an integrated regional platform at 6–8× ⚠ .....  ~$8.4M – $11.2M
```

The AI grows EBITDA by roughly a third. **Consolidation roughly doubles the
multiple applied to it.** Both matter, but the second one is bigger and does not
depend on our technology working. That asymmetry should shape everything: the AI
is what funds debt service, justifies the platform narrative, and de-risks the
hold — the *return* comes substantially from having assembled the thing at all.

### 3.2 The honest risks

**Licensing is the existential dependency.** If a qualifier walks, operations
stop. This is a categorically different risk from a normal key-employee problem.
Mitigation: give the qualifier real equity from day one (SPEC.md §7.1/S7), build
a second qualifier before deal 3, and confirm exact license classes and transfer
mechanics with the State Fire Marshal *before* making an offer, not during
diligence.

**Consolidators have bid up the good mid-size shops** ⚠. Stay at $150–400k SDE,
below their acquisition screen. Our advantage is at a size they will not bother
with; it disappears above roughly $500k SDE.

**Sprinkler inspection needs certified inspectors** (NICET or equivalent) who are
scarce and expensive. Start with the extinguisher / alarm / emergency-lighting
segment, which has a far shallower certification requirement, and add sprinkler
capability at deal 3 once there is scale to support it.

**Deficiency chasing has an ethical floor and it is a hard constraint.** The
revenue engine described above works because the deficiencies are real and
legally required. The moment it becomes pressure to sell unnecessary work, it is
a safety business selling fear — reputationally fatal, potentially a licensing
matter, and genuinely wrong. Hard rules: the system only ever follows up on
deficiencies a technician actually documented with a photo, never generates or
suggests deficiencies, and quotes only code-required corrections. Build this as
an enforced constraint in the software, not a policy.

**Recurring revenue is real but re-earned annually.** These are not multi-year
contracts in most segments — they are annual cycles with very high natural
retention *if you call*. Which is exactly why the due-date engine is the whole
business, and also why the base is recoverable when a tired owner stops calling.

### 3.3 Second choice: Commercial pest control with termite

If fire proves unsourceable in our metro, this is the fallback and it is close.

Better than fire on: deal availability (far more targets), operational
simplicity, and the Ch. 482 Certified Operator requirement is a comparably good
moat with a clearer path to satisfying it. Termite bonds are annual renewals with
excellent retention, and the missed-call problem in pest is severe — these
businesses lose a large share of inbound calls, and every missed call is a
customer who calls the next listing.

Worse than fire on: demand is largely elective rather than mandated (the big
exception being WDO inspections for real-estate transactions and pest programs
required for commercial food service), and there is no deficiency-report
mechanic, so the AI lever is meaningfully smaller.

Run it as the declared fallback. Do not run both.

### 3.4 The sleeper: generator / emergency power PM

Underrated and very Florida-specific. Post-Irma, Florida requires backup power
capability at nursing homes and assisted living facilities ⚠, on top of the
existing hospital, data center, telecom, and fuel-station demand. Mandated,
recurring PM contracts, highly fragmented, and almost nobody is consolidating it
at the small end.

Fails only on deal availability — the universe of pure-play shops at our size is
thin. **Best use: a service line to bolt onto the fire platform at deal 3+,**
where it shares the customer (the same facility manager buys both), the same
compliance-documentation workflow, and the same route.

That is the strategic shape worth noticing: fire is not just a niche, it is a
**platform that other mandated-compliance service lines bolt onto** — backflow,
generator PM, fire door inspection, emergency lighting. One relationship with a
facility manager, one compliance calendar, one document system, many service
lines. Each addition raises revenue per customer with near-zero acquisition cost.

## 4. GEOGRAPHY — ONE METRO, NOT A STATE

"Florida" is not a strategy; route density is the economics and it is local.

**Recommendation: Tampa Bay**, with Orlando as the alternative.

Dense commercial and industrial base, deep bench of long-tenured owner-operators,
enough targets to run a five-deal program inside a 45-minute radius, and less
exposure to seasonal-transient population dynamics than South or Southwest
Florida. Orlando trades some of that for hospitality-sector concentration, which
cuts both ways — enormous mandated-inspection demand, more cyclical customers.

Rule: **every acquisition after the first must be within 45 minutes of the
first.** Outside that radius, the route synergy is imaginary and we are just
buying unrelated businesses.

## 5. THE FIVE-DEAL PATH

**Pre-deal-zero (weeks 1–8), before making any offer.** Solve licensing. Confirm
exact FL license classes, transfer mechanics, and qualifier requirements with the
State Fire Marshal. Identify and sign a qualifier — an experienced licensed
operator, ideally someone near retirement who wants equity and part-time
involvement rather than a salary. **Nothing else matters until this exists.**
Simultaneously, build the target list from public records (SPEC.md §3.1) —
licensed fire equipment dealers cross-referenced against entity age, officer
continuity, and UCC filings. The licensed universe is enumerable, which makes
this the rare niche where you can build a *complete* map of every target.

**Deal 1 (months 3–9). Write the playbook.** Target the largest one we can
finance, $250–400k SDE. Bigger is better here: fixed overhead of the AI build
spreads over a larger base, and there is more slack to absorb our mistakes.
Expect six months, not three. The deliverable is not the business — it is the
documented playbook and the software.

**Deal 2 (months 9–13). Validate it.** Deliberately smaller, $150–250k SDE, and
within 45 minutes. This deal answers the only question that matters: was deal 1 a
playbook or a coincidence? Measure the same improvements against the same
baselines (SPEC.md §10). If deal 2's realized numbers do not resemble deal 1's,
stop acquiring and find out why before deal 3.

**Deals 3–5 (months 13–30). Compound.** Cadence approaches three months. Finance
increasingly against holdco capacity rather than target standalone economics
(SPEC.md §7.1/S11) — this is where founder capital genuinely reaches zero. Add
adjacent mandated service lines. Consolidate the back office. Build the second
qualifier.

**Month 30+. Decide.** Hold for cash flow, or present an integrated regional
platform to the consolidators at a materially higher multiple than any component
was bought for.

## 6. WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE PRODUCT

Choosing a niche makes the software dramatically better, because a general
acquisition tool becomes a specific one.

1. **The buy box becomes concrete.** Fire & life safety, Tampa Bay, $150–400k SDE,
   licensed under Ch. 633, within 45 minutes of the platform.

2. **Sourcing becomes enumerable rather than probabilistic.** The State Fire
   Marshal licensee list *is* the complete target universe. Cross-reference it
   against Sunbiz entity age, officer continuity, and UCC filings and you have a
   ranked map of every acquisition candidate in the metro. This is a fundamentally
   better position than screening listings, and it is only possible in a licensed
   vertical. **It is arguably the strongest single argument for this niche.**

3. **The AI upside model stops being generic.** Replace the abstract category
   library with three modeled, measurable levers specific to this business:
   lapsed-base recovery, deficiency-to-quote conversion, and price realization.
   Each has a real baseline extractable from the seller's system during diligence
   — device counts, due dates, historical deficiency reports, conversion history.
   **This is what turns SPEC.md §6's "measured inputs only" rule from a
   restriction into something we can actually satisfy.**

4. **Diligence becomes a specific, high-signal checklist.** Device count under
   contract; due-date distribution; lapsed customer count and age; historical
   deficiency conversion rate; license classes held and by whom; qualifier
   retention risk; recurring vs project revenue split; top-customer concentration
   among property-management groups.

5. **The 90-day plan becomes a real, reusable, improving artifact** rather than a
   generated template — and it gets better with every acquisition. That is the
   compounding asset.

6. **The predicted-vs-realized loop (SPEC.md §10) finally has comparable units.**
   Five businesses in one vertical with the same three levers produce a genuine
   calibration dataset. Five businesses in five verticals produce five anecdotes.

**A caution worth stating.** The vertical software we build will look like a
sellable SaaS product for the fire protection industry. It probably is one. Do
not chase it — it is a different company with different economics, and pursuing
it will starve the acquisition thesis of the attention that is its actual
constraint. Note it as exit optionality and leave it alone until the platform is
built.
